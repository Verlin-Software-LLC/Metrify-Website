How it works

The fitness app you can just tell what you ate

Plenty of apps listen now. The question worth asking is what happens after they hear you.

Published 31 July 2026

No manual logging is now table stakes

If you are looking for a calorie tracker where you describe your food instead of hunting through a database, you have options. Photo logging is widespread. Voice input has arrived in the big incumbents. A number of newer apps let you type a sentence and get a logged meal.

So "you can just tell it what you ate" no longer separates anything. Almost everyone can take the sentence. What separates them is what the app is able to do with your log once it has understood you, and that difference is invisible until about day three, when you make your first mistake.

The three questions that actually sort these apps

1. Can it log a whole meal from one sentence?

Real meals are not one item. Dinner is chicken, rice, broccoli, and a drink. Some apps take your sentence and then walk you through each item individually, which is the old search-and-confirm loop wearing a new coat. The test is whether "chicken breast, rice, broccoli, and a diet coke" produces four logged entries from one message.

Metrify logs all four, and shows you a single card with what it understood, so you confirm once instead of four times.

2. Can it fix what it just wrote?

This is the question almost nobody asks before subscribing, and the one that decides whether you are still using the app in a month.

You will misspeak. You will say three eggs when you had two. An app whose assistant can only produce new entries will respond to "actually make that two eggs" by logging a second egg entry, leaving you to go find the first one and delete it manually. You have now done more work than if you had typed it into a search box.

An assistant with real access to your log edits the row it already wrote. In Metrify, "actually make that two eggs, not three" corrects the existing entry. Nothing to clean up.

3. Can it read your log back to you?

A tracker you can talk to should be able to answer questions about the thing it is tracking. "How much protein do I have left today?" and "was I more consistent this week or last?" are the whole point of having a conversational interface in the first place.

Many AI features in fitness apps are advice layers. They will discuss protein in general terms because they cannot see your data. Metrify's assistant queries your actual logged history and answers from it.

Why the distinction exists at all

It comes down to whether the assistant has tools or just a text box.

The common pattern is a language model bolted onto a conventional tracking app. It can generate text, and somewhere behind it a simple handler turns "I ate a sandwich" into one new database row. It cannot edit, cannot look anything up, and cannot touch the rest of the app.

Metrify's assistant has a catalog of tools it can call against your database: search foods, write single and bulk entries, edit entries, save custom foods, log exercise and workout sessions, and read daily summaries and history. That is why it can do all three things above, and why a whole day of food, corrections, training, and questions fits in one conversation.

The other thing to check: who has your data

Every app in this category is processing descriptions of what you eat, your weight, and often your training. That is a detailed picture of your body and your habits, and in most of these products it lives on someone else's servers under a policy that can change.

Metrify stores every food log, weigh-in, workout, and goal locally on your device. Delete the app and it is gone, because it was never anywhere else. That extends to what the assistant remembers about you: tell it once that you are vegetarian or eating gluten free and it carries that forward in every conversation, held on your phone.

And one thing most of them get wrong

Worth checking before you commit to any tracker: what happens to your calorie goal when you log a workout.

Most raise it. You burned 400 calories, so you may now eat 400 more. That is a documented, deliberate design in several major apps, and it can usually be switched off. The trouble is that it interacts badly with the activity level you picked during setup. If you chose "active" because you train four times a week, your baseline already contains those sessions, and logging them counts the same calories twice. Your real deficit is smaller than the number on screen, nothing warns you, and the symptom is a stall you cannot explain.

Metrify uses a sedentary baseline with no activity level question, and counts logged exercise exactly once. Your goal does not move when you train. The full argument is in why your workout should count once.

A short checklist

If you are comparing apps in this category, these are the things to actually test during a trial:

  • Log a four-item meal in one sentence. Did it get all four?
  • Immediately correct one item. Did it edit, or did it add a duplicate?
  • Ask what you have left for the day. Did it answer from your data, or give generic advice?
  • Ask it to repeat yesterday's lunch. Did it find it?
  • Log a workout in the same conversation. Did it work at all?
  • Check where the app says your data is stored.
  • Log a workout and watch whether your calorie goal moves.
